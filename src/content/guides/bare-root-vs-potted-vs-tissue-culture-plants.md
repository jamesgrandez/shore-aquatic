# Bare-Root vs Potted vs Tissue Culture Aquarium Plants

**Quick answer:** **Bare-root bundles** give you the most plants for the money and are how the aquarium trade has always moved stem plants. **Potted plants** cost more per plant but are easy to handle and good for rooted plants. **Tissue culture** cups are lab-grown, cost the most per plant, and are the cleanest option if you can't risk any hitchhikers (for example, a prized shrimp colony). For filling a new tank on a budget, bare-root wins.

## The comparison at a glance

| | Bare-root bundles | Potted plants | Tissue culture (TC) cups |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What arrives** | Stems or cuttings held with a band, packed damp. No pot, no gravel | Plant in a small plastic basket with rock wool around the roots | Small plantlets in a sealed cup of clear nutrient gel |
| **Typical plants per purchase** | Several stems per bundle | One plant (sometimes a few small ones) | One cup, often many tiny plantlets |
| **Cost per plant** | Lowest | Medium to high | Highest for its size |
| **Size on arrival** | Full-size stems, ready to plant | Full-size plant | Small; takes weeks to fill in |
| **Chance of hitchhikers** (a tiny snail, duckweed) | Possible: nursery-grown, so rinse before planting | Possible: rock wool can hide them | Very low: grown in sterile lab conditions |
| **Melting / leaf drop after planting** | Common, temporary | Common, temporary | Also happens; tiny plants are fragile at first |
| **Work to plant** | Cut the band, separate the stems, plant each one | Remove pot and all rock wool, then plant | Wash off all the gel, plant in small clumps |
| **Best for** | Filling a tank fast, stem plants, budgets, beginners | Swords, crypts and other rooted plants; gifts | Carpets, shrimp tanks, keeping a tank completely clean |

## Bare-root bundles

This is how most stem plants have always been sold to fish stores: cut from the grower's beds, bunched, banded and shipped damp.

**Why they cost less:** you aren't paying for a pot, rock wool, or the extra weeks a plant spends in a pot before sale. That's why our [9-bundle variety pack](/shop/plant-variety-pack-9-bundles) costs about what a single potted plant does at many stores.

**What to know:**
- Each bundle is several stems. Separate them before planting. A banded clump planted whole holds water in the middle and rots from the inside.
- Stem plants root from the cut, so a soft stem end isn't a dead plant. Trim back to firm green tissue and plant the healthy top.
- Nursery stock can carry a little duckweed or a tiny snail. Rinse every plant before it goes in the tank.

**Choose bare-root if:** you're setting up a new tank, want a jungle look without spending a fortune, or mostly want stem plants.

## Potted plants

Potted aquarium plants sit in a plastic basket packed with rock wool, a mineral fiber that holds the roots.

**What to know:**
- **Take all the rock wool off.** Left on, it traps waste around the roots and the plant often struggles. Tease it out gently with your fingers or tweezers.
- You can often split one pot into several plants once the rock wool is off.
- Pots are handy for rooted plants like swords and crypts, because the root ball is intact.

**Choose potted if:** you want a few specific showpiece plants, you're buying rooted plants, or you prefer the convenience.

## Tissue culture (TC) plants

TC plants are grown from tiny pieces of plant in a sterile lab, then sealed in a cup of nutrient gel.

![Plant tissue culture in a laboratory](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Plant_tissue_culture.jpg?width=1200)
*Tissue culture plants are grown in sterile containers in a lab, which is why they're so clean.*

**What to know:**
- Because they're grown sterile, they're the least likely to bring snails, algae or pesticide residue.
- They're small. A cup that looks full is usually a mat of tiny plantlets that need weeks to grow into a tank.
- Rinse off every bit of gel before planting, or it will cloud the water and feed algae.

**Choose TC if:** you keep sensitive shrimp, you're growing a carpet, or you need a tank to stay completely free of hitchhikers.

## "Emersed" vs "submersed": why plants change after planting

Many aquarium plants are farmed with their leaves **out of the water** (emersed), because they grow faster in air and don't get algae. Once they're underwater, they grow a new kind of leaf suited to life under water and drop some of the old ones.

![Water wisteria showing different leaves grown in air and under water](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Hygrophila_difformis_also_known_as_water_wisteria_Submerged_and_Emmersed_growth_2.jpg?width=1000)
*The same water wisteria plant: thick leaves grown in air and finer leaves grown under water.*

This happens with bare-root, potted and TC plants alike. A few yellowing or melting older leaves in the first couple of weeks is normal, and new growth follows. Our guide to [plant melt](/blog/aquarium-plants-melting) explains what to trim and what to leave.

## FAQ

**Are bare-root plants lower quality?**
No. It's the same plant without the pot. The difference is packaging and price.

**Will bare-root plants survive shipping?**
Yes, when packed damp and kept at a safe temperature. We wrap plants in moisture-holding material, seal them in bags and ship in insulated boxes with heat or cool packs as the weather needs. See [how we ship live plants in winter and summer](/guides/shipping-live-plants-winter-summer).

**Do I need to quarantine new plants?**
At minimum, rinse every plant well and look it over for snails or eggs. Shrimp keepers often keep new plants in a separate container for a week or two first.

**Can I plant rock wool?**
Don't. Remove it completely before planting.
